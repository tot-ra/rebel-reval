## At a glance
- Walks the street with a rolling, wide-legged gait, as if the cobbles were a deck; he crossed the Baltic as a ship's boy before he ever kept a ledger.
- Ledger first, conversation second: his sentences are short and numbered.
- Lends grain and silver to men who cannot refuse him, and never names the interest.
- Keeps a dozen hens in the yard behind the warehouse; they are his only extravagance.

## Biography
Erik was born in 1313 in Åbo, in the Finnish coast of the Swedish crown, the son of Folke Eriksson, a grain-and-tar shipper who moved his business to Reval in 1322 when the Åbo harbour tolls rose. Folke died in the winter of 1338; the warehouse on Suur-Kloostri, the debts owed to him and the dozen trading friends who owed him favours came to Erik at twenty-five.

At seventeen he served a season on a cog out of Visby and carried the burn from it. The lesson he took was that goods are cheap where a ship lands and dear where a man needs them, and that the gap is a living. He married Ulrika Larsdotter in 1340; their daughter Kristina was born in the spring of 1342.

He is not loved, but he is dependable: a cart ordered of Erik arrives on the day he said or he sends a boy to say why. He lives now above his own warehouse with a household of twelve, and he sleeps badly after quarter-days.

## Motivation
- **Want:** A seat in the Kindergilde and a council bench for his daughter's husband to inherit. He prices everything toward that.
- **Fear:** A bad shipping year that calls in his own credit before his debtors have paid.
- **Contradiction:** He is gentle with the hens and the infant, merciless with a late repayment; he thinks both are simply good housekeeping.
- **Secret or withheld fact:** Part of his cheap stock comes from Finnish coasters that pay no toll at the Coastal Gate because a cousin's skipper takes the wrong channel. He tells himself it is custom. It is smuggling.

## Work and money
Erik buys rye, salt, tar and Finnish hides at the quay and sells to bakers, brewers and the shipwrights of Aida. Margins run about one part in six when the ships come on time. He carries perhaps 140 marks out on credit, including 12 marks of grain to Johannes Fromme and 15 marks to Peeter Tanieli poeg; none of it is in the Rat's books. A bad month is a late ship and a bad wind: then he calls in the small loans first.

## Faction and belief
He has no faction and wants none: every banner in Reval means a levy on warehouses. If asked for a small favour he gives it, for a large one he asks the price, and to inform he would first ask who benefits. He keeps the feasts of St Olaf and says the Swedish grace at table; he nails a rowan twig above the hen-house door and does not discuss it.

## Voice
- **Registers:** Swedish at home and with skippers; Middle Low German at the quay and in the Rat's hall; Latin for charters, badly pronounced.
- **Delivery:** Clipped and precise, quick, volume low; he gives numbers before names.
- **Sample lines:** "Twelve marks, grain, Michaelmas. That was the word." / "Wat kost dat, un wanner? Nicht anners." / "Your hens, Henneke. My beer. Same as last time."
- **Verbal tic:** Ends statements with "so."

## Knowledge and rumours
He knows which skippers short-weight, the true price of rye on three quays, and which councillors are in debt. He would trade it for a safer berth for his ships. He believes the false rumour that the viceroy plans a toll on every warehouse door.
