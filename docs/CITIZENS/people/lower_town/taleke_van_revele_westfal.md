## At a glance
- Low, unhurried voice that does not change when a child screams or a bailiff knocks.
- Stands at the stream with her sleeves rolled, wringing sheets and counting them.
- Her byname marks Westphalian stock settled in Reval, as it does Sophia in the van Deventer house; they are not kin.
- Works harder than her husband admits and tells no one how little he sees of it.

## Biography
Born in 1309 in Reval, the daughter of a Westphalian rope-maker, Taleke married Albert van Paderborne ([card](albert_van_paderborne.md)) in 1328. She bore six children and buried a seventh. As the shop outgrew the household she took in washing from merchants on Pikk to keep the maids from idleness; the money is hers by custom. She was drawn to the circle by the clerk Kersten, who spoke kindly of the washerwomen's fraternity at a christening, and who now asks for small words. She does not tell Albert what she hears.

## Motivation
- **Want:** To keep her children fed and out of service.
- **Fear:** Another baby too soon, and the damp that took her seventh.
- **Contradiction:** Thrifty, yet lends the maids soap from her own supply.
- **Secret or withheld fact:** She knows which merchants' linen is stained with wine and blood, and keeps count.

## Work and money
She earns about one mark a month from washing, paid in pfennigs by the merchants, and keeps it in a pot beneath the bed. Lye and ash she buys from the soap-boilers; hard soap is dear. A bad month is a wet one that rots the linen on the line.

## Faction and belief
Hanseatic sympathy: she wants the merchants paying on time and the gates open for the carts that bring her linen. Her reason is bread, not politics. She would pass a small word, refuse to carry anything heavy and inform only on someone who threatened her children. She keeps St Agatha's bread on the shelf against fire and prays at St Nicholas.

## Voice
- **Registers:** Low German in the house; Estonian with washerwomen.
- **Delivery:** Low and unhurried; she talks while she works.
- **Sample lines:** "Count the sheets again, child." "Words cost nothing and everything." "Dat is nu so."
- **Verbal tic:** "Gently, now."

## Knowledge and rumours
She knows who in the quarter has a sick child by the state of their linen. She would trade it for a favour at the stream. She believes the false rumour that soap will be taxed at midsummer.
