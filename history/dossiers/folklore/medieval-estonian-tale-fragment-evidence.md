# Medieval Estonian tale-fragment evidence pass (R-075)

## Brief for Canon Keeper and Narrative

This note tests whether a securely dated medieval or fourteenth-century Estonian tale fragment, sermon, chronicle passage, or Reval/Harju record can replace one later-recording composite in [`tales-tellable-in-1343.md`](./tales-tellable-in-1343.md). It is a source-discovery note, not a promotion of folklore to canon.

**Verdict:** no qualifying new tale fragment was located in the institutional catalogue and scholarly source pass below. The current confidence assignments should remain unchanged. T-01 and T-02 already use the relevant medieval chronicle material; the later-recorded T-03-T-08 plots remain **`folklore`** or **`plausible composite`**, not **`attested`**.

## Search boundary and findings

### 1. Estonian Folklore Archives / Estonian Literary Museum anthology

The English summary of *Estonian Folktales I: 1. Fairy Tales* describes the scholarly edition as drawing on the Estonian Folklore Archives' manuscript corpus and says that it represents Estonian fairy-tale recordings written down over two hundred years. Its earliest dated text is **"The Magic Purse" (ATU 562), published in *Beiträge* in 1816** [1]. This is a catalogue boundary for recorded fairy tales, not proof that no medieval oral story existed.

The same summary distinguishes fairy tales from legends and folk-belief notes, and warns that the earlier written recordings do not reveal how a teller presented a borderline legend/fairy-tale plot [1]. Therefore a modern tale-type match or a later oral variant cannot be upgraded into a fourteenth-century Reval/Harju witness without a dated medieval text.

### 2. Folklore.ee diachronic legend study

Mare Kalda's institutional *Folklore* article analyses "The Whining Shinbone" (*Vinguv jalaluu*) as a legend in Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald's *Eesti rahva ennemuistsed jutud*. The article dates the anthology's first edition to **1866** and records the earliest relevant textualisation in a **1862** letter/variant context, while discussing later editions and oral reproductions [2]. This is useful evidence for the recording horizon of a named legend, but it is not a fourteenth-century witness and cannot replace a late-recording composite in the 1343 catalogue.

### 3. Medieval material found, but not a new replacement candidate

The medieval narrative evidence already represented by T-01 and T-02 remains Henry of Livonia's *Chronicon Livoniae* material on Tharapita and the destruction of an image, reported through a Christian missionary lens [3]. The present pass found no separate dated fourteenth-century Estonian-language tale, sermon, or Reval/Harju civic record that preserves a tellable plot. The absence is limited to the sources and catalogue boundaries reviewed here; it is not a claim that every archive or unpublished manuscript has been exhaustively searched.

## Open questions and access boundary

- A direct Tallinn City Archives / National Archives search of unpublished Reval registers, sermons, and manuscript fragments could still find a short vernacular or Latin anecdote; no such primary folio was accessed in this pass.
- The catalogue result concerns recorded fairy tales. A future specialist pass may separately search medieval miracle collections, exempla, legal depositions, and chronicles for story-like episodes that are not classified as fairy tales.
- Any candidate must preserve its original source language, date, genre, and mediation. A medieval mention of a deity or ritual is not automatically a locally current folk tale.
