# Tales tellable in Reval and Harjumaa, spring 1343

## Brief for Narrative and Dialogue

This dossier is a **speaking catalogue**, not a claim that the listed plots were written down in
1343. A character may tell a tale in 1343 when its oral layer is regionally plausible, while the
scholarly record date stays visible to the production team.

1. Use the seven entries in the main table as short barks, fireside stories, work songs, or clerical
   exempla. Each names a teller, audience, setting, and playable speaking length.
2. **`attested`** means the narrative is reported in a medieval source; it does not make the source's
   missionary interpretation neutral. The fairy tales and spirit plots are **`folklore`** because
   their surviving records are later collections of oral tradition [1][3][4][5].
3. A 1343 teller should use a local frame: a barn, road, grove, harbour, sick-bed, or churchyard.
   Do not make a character cite Kreutzwald, Eisen, Loorits, or a modern legend collector.
4. Estonian tellers can treat spirits as neighbours and warnings; a burgher or Dominican can repeat
   the same plot as theft, fraud, or diabolical superstition [6].
5. The **late-recording quarantine** is intentional. The Ülemiste Elder, Linda's Toompea story, and
   Olev's church-builder tale are useful later folklore, but must not be presented as attested local
   speech in April-May 1343 [6][7].

## Findings

### Dating rule for oral tales

The surviving written record for most Estonian tales is nineteenth century or later. That date is
evidence for the tale's documented form, not proof that the same wording existed in 1343. A plot is
usable in the game only as a **`folklore`** oral layer or, when several motifs are combined for one
speaker, a **`plausible composite`**; the dossier must preserve the later recording horizon [1][3][4].
The medieval exceptions below are still source-mediated: Henry of Livonia reports Tharapita and an
idol-destruction episode through a Christian missionary lens [2].

### Delivery and dating constraints

- A tale should sound **current** when the teller uses a first-person frame (“my aunt heard it at the
  mill”) or a local place and social problem, while the script notes retain the later collector date.
  This is a **plausible composite**, not a fabricated claim that the collector's wording existed in
  1343 [1][3].
- Rural Estonian speech may leave the spirit morally ambiguous. A German burgher can translate the
  kratt into a theft accusation; a Dominican can call it superstition or devilry. Both are in-world
  readings of the same **folklore** material [6].
- Do not give all seven tales to one omniscient lore-giver. T-01 and T-08 belong to regional identity;
  T-03 and T-06 to household labour; T-04 and T-05 to comic or monster entertainment; T-07 to
  children, couriers, and animal keepers.
- The exact length is a dialogue-planning estimate, not a historical measurement. One spoken line is
  roughly 5-7 seconds; pauses, audience interruption, and a song refrain can extend a scene.

### Late-recording quarantine

These are good later folklore but should not be presented as securely current, locally established
1343 tales without an explicit narrator's uncertainty:

| Motif | Why it is quarantined | Safe use |
|---|---|---|
| **Ülemiste Elder** asking whether the city is finished | A later Tallinn legend; the existing folklore research explicitly rejects it as a 1343 attested custom [6]. | A late-game rumour marked **`folklore`**, or a modern/retrospective reference layer; not a factual guard ritual. |
| **Linda's tears and Toompea as Kalev's grave** | A Kalevipoeg literary synthesis recorded in the nineteenth century [3][6]. | A rebel's poetic interpretation, clearly **`folklore`**, not a discovered historical foundation story. |
| **Olev and the toad at St Olaf's** | A later church-builder legend attached to a real landmark [6]. | A mason's spooky story with an explicit “old people say” frame; never an attested 1343 biography. |

## Production hooks

- **Narrative:** Use T-01 for rural identity and the difference between chronicle memory and living
  belief. Use T-03 for a moral choice about wealth or theft, T-04 for class satire, T-06 for grief,
  and T-07 for secrets overheard in a living city. Keep each plot a self-contained oral beat rather
  than a new quest unless a task activates it [1][3][6].
- **Dialogue:** Give Ellen, Aita, Kaja, Mart, and Urmas different delivery modes. Ellen can pause on a
  warning; Aita can ground a spirit tale in food and household work; Kaja can compress a plot into a
  courier's bark; Mart can question the supernatural reading; Urmas can connect T-01 to rebellion.
  Use Estonian names only where the speaker would plausibly know them; do not quote nineteenth-century
  Estonian literary prose as medieval dialogue [3][6].
- **Quest / Narrative:** T-03 can motivate suspicion around missing grain, but the kratt remains
  **folklore**, not proof of a supernatural crime. T-04 can be a work-competition anecdote, T-06 a
  grief or household-choice scene, and T-07 an investigation device only after a separate quest task
  authorises it. The catalogue itself activates no quest seed [6].
- **Character:** Match tale and social location: labourers and rural visitors carry T-01/T-03/T-04;
  healers and household elders carry T-05/T-06; couriers and animal workers carry T-07; islanders
  carry T-08. A German or clerical character may tell the same story as evidence against a suspect
  rather than as belief [6].
- **Systems:** Store `tale_id`, `teller_role`, `setting`, `delivery_seconds`, and `confidence` as
  dialogue metadata if these barks are implemented. `confidence` should remain visible to content
  review and must not become a player-facing “truth” flag.

## Open questions

- Determine whether an Estonian-language specialist can identify a securely dated medieval or
  fourteenth-century tale fragment that would replace one of the later-recording composites.
- Compare regional variants of the kratt and *Vanapagan* plots from Harju, Viru, and Saaremaa before
  assigning a specific variant to a named NPC.
- Establish whether any 1340-1343 Reval or Harju record mentions public storytelling, professional
  performers, or restrictions on night gatherings; otherwise keep the catalogue as informal speech.
- Audit every future dialogue use of T-01 to T-08 so a later-recorded tale is labelled **`folklore`**
  or **`plausible composite`** in its content metadata and is not presented as an attested event.
