# Games and leisure of common people in Reval and its villages (spring 1343)

Task **R-1957** (PT-1) of the pastimes epic **R-1956**. The game design that uses it is
[`docs/SYSTEMS/PASTIMES.md`](../../../docs/SYSTEMS/PASTIMES.md).

Extends [`festivals-games-and-public-life`](../culture/festivals-games-and-public-life.md), which
sets the feast windows, the forum and tavern settings and the siege rule (after 23 April alarms,
ration queues and musters displace leisure). This dossier answers its open question on local game
finds: dice move from `plausible composite` to **attested** for Reval.

Labels: **A** attested in Reval or Livonia; **B** attested in Hanseatic or Baltic towns, or in
Estonian archaeology of the period; **C** plausible composite; **D** invented; **X** later than
1343, exclude. Period rule: dated later -> exclude; unknown 1343 state -> simplified later form,
labelled.

## Brief for dev, art and map

1. **Dice are the best-attested adult pastime in Reval.** 14 cube dice came out of the 1953
   excavation of the Town Hall Square (Raekoja plats), most of bone, one of antler, 7-10 mm a side,
   pips as ring-and-dot, and opposite faces numbered **1:2, 3:4, 5:6** (the northern European
   arrangement of the 13th-16th centuries, not the modern "sum to 7") **A** [1]. About 30 dice are
   known from Estonia in all, mostly medieval, one of amber from Tallinn **A** [2].
2. **People cheated.** One Town Hall Square die has its two highest faces made slightly larger so it
   lands on them more often; loaded dice with lead under a pip are known elsewhere **A** (Reval die)
   [1]. Dice were played for stakes, in company, at taverns and meeting places; the square, laid out
   around 1300, was such a place **A/B** [1].
3. **Church and council disapproved.** Statutes against `dobbelen` / `dobbelspil` survive for Tallinn,
   Riga, Tartu and Pärnu; Tallinn court books fine gamblers ("de dobbeler") one mark or half a mark
   and punish cheats far harder; gaming on Sundays and feast days is forbidden. The surviving Tallinn
   records run **1394-1521**, so the exact 1343 regime is unknown: show disapproval, a watchman's
   warning or a fine, but do not quote a 1343 statute **B (later-attested form)** [3].
4. **Gamblers were everyone:** sailors and skippers, young men, journeymen, apprentices, alehouse
   keepers, foreigners, and guild merchants **B** [3].
5. **The commonest toy in Estonian finds is the buzz bone** (`vurriluu`): a pig's foot bone with a
   hole, on a doubled string twisted and pulled so it spins and hums. Known from the Viking Age
   through medieval towns and villages to the 20th century **B** [2].
6. **Children** also had knucklebones (sheep/goat/cattle/pig astragali, from Lihula and Otepää among
   other sites; first an adults' dice, later a children's game) **B** [1]; four-sided spinning tops
   with pips that double as dice **B** [4]; a ball of rolled leather strips (Tartu Town Hall Square)
   **B** [2]; tiny clay pots for play **B** [2]. Lübeck, the mother town of Reval's law, has over 700
   toy finds: wooden swords, daggers, axes, bows, dolls with moving limbs, animal figures **B** [2].
   Most Estonian toys were wood, birch bark or rush and do not survive, so absence of finds is not
   absence of play [2].
7. **"Was there football?" No evidence for Livonia.** Mob football is an English and French
   phenomenon of the 13th-14th centuries; nothing comparable is recorded for Reval or the Hanseatic
   Baltic. What we can show: children throwing or kicking a stitched leather ball (the Tartu find)
   **B**, and, in the villages, the folk game of driving a wooden "egg" or ball with sticks
   (`ämmamuna`, `munamäng`), which Estonian runic songs tie to spring feasts and the dead; it is
   recorded only in the 19th-20th centuries, so it is a **folklore / simplified later form C**
   [5][6].
8. **Nine men's morris** (`veskimäng`) was one of the most popular board games of
   medieval Europe; Tallinn City Museum teaches it as a medieval game **B**. A morris board or piece
   from Estonia is **not confirmed** by this pass (see Open questions) [4][7].
9. **Exclude:** popinjay and marksmen's companies (Riga's company is early 15th century) **X** [8];
   Blackheads (Reval 1399) **X**; merchant-guild feasts with a May Count, popinjay and carnival
   processions are studied from 1350 and mostly documented later, so no guild festival scenes in
   1343 **X/U** [9]; late-medieval clay cockerel whistles **X** [2]; hopscotch (no Estonian medieval
   source) **X** [2]; playing cards (not in the Baltic by 1343) **X**.
10. **Easter 1343 fell on 13 April**, ten days before St George's Night. Easter opens the swinging
    season in Estonian folk custom (village swings built by young men, with songs, games and
    strength trials); the evidence is 19th-century, the 1343 form is unknown, so a simple timber
    village swing at Easter is a **simplified later form C** [6][10].
11. **After 23 April the siege thins leisure:** keep a few dice games among watchmen and children's
    play, drop feast-day swings and village games near the rising, per the festivals dossier.

## Findings

### Dice and gaming

- Cube dice: Town Hall Square 1953, 14 dice, bone and one antler, 7-10 mm, ring-and-dot pips, several
  pips cut with the same tool (perhaps bought as a set from one maker); opposite faces 1:2, 3:4, 5:6
  **A** [1].
- Knucklebones as primitive dice at late prehistoric hillforts (Soontagana, Varbola) and medieval
  castles and settlements (Lihula, Otepää) **B** [1].
- Dice rules varied: stakes were laid on combinations or on the total thrown; Alfonso X's *Libro de
  los juegos* (1283) records several 13th-century dice games **B** [1]. No Low German rule text for
  Reval is known to this pass. The German word *Pasch* (a throw of doubles or triples) is the
  standard gamblers' term and gives a safe core for a reconstructed game **C**.
- Regulation and gamblers: see Brief 3-4 [3]. Gaming tied to drink led to knife fights; the court
  books record a 1435 case of grabbing a knife over a game and 1412 / 1458 cases of keeping gaming in
  a house or cellar **B (later)** [3]. Public burning of dice and boards is reported in Europe **B**
  [1].

### Board games

- Nine men's morris: 24 points on three nested squares joined at the side mid-points; nine men
  each; place, then move along lines; three in a row ("mill", `veski`) removes an enemy man not in a
  mill unless none other remains; fewer than three men or no legal move loses [4]. Popular across
  medieval Europe, boards scratched on stone and wood **B** [7].
- Tables (backgammon ancestors) and chess are attested for medieval Scandinavia and Germany; chess
  belongs to castles and the rich. No Estonian find confirmed in this pass **U**.

### Children's play

- Buzz bones, knucklebones, four-sided tops, leather ball, miniature pots, toy weapons (Lübeck), see
  Brief 5-6 [2][4].
- Tag (`kull`, `läts`) and similar running games are universal and recorded from the 1930s; show
  them as generic chasing **C** [6].

### Village customs and contests

- Spring ball-and-stick game (`ämmamuna`), wheel rolling (`rattamäng`) on village lanes: folk records
  of the 19th-20th centuries with ritual readings; **C, folklore** [5][6].
- Easter swing season with games and strength trials: 19th-century evidence **C** [10].
- Wrestling, stick-pulling (`vägikaika vedu`) and finger-pulling are well known Estonian folk
  contests, but this pass found **no source dating them** **U**.

### Shooting and guild festivals

- Riga: Guild of the Holy Spirit split into Great and Small Guild in 1354; marksmen's company early
  15th century **X for 1343** [8]. Merchant-guild festivals (Christmas, Carnival, popinjay, May Count)
  in Livonian towns are studied for 1350-1550 [9].

## Production hooks

- **Art:** bone dice 7-10 mm with ring-and-dot pips and 1:2 / 3:4 / 5:6 faces (one antler die a
  little larger); leather dice cup is **C**; buzz bone on a doubled string; knucklebones; four-sided
  wooden top with pips; rolled leather-strip ball; a morris board cut into a plank or a bench top;
  wooden toy sword and bow; a plain timber village swing (two posts, cross beam, plank seat).
- **Map:** dice at the Town Hall Square edge, by alehouses and on the harbour; children in lanes and
  yards; swing and ball-and-stick on a Harju village green in Easter week.
- **Character:** dice for journeymen, sailors, carters, apprentices, alehouse keepers (men, adult);
  morris for artisans and older men; buzz bones, tops and toy weapons for boys and girls; swings for
  village youth of both sexes.
- **Quest / Narrative:** a cheat with an "improved" die; a watchman breaking up dice on a feast day;
  Easter-week swing a few days before St George's Night.
- **Dialogue:** `dobbelen` (to dice), `dobbeler` (gambler), *Pasch* (doubles), `veski` (mill),
  `vurr` (buzz bone / top), `kiik` (swing).
- **Dev / systems:** dice game is pure luck with a cheat option; morris is a solved game, so authored
  AI tiers are cheap; buzz bone is a skill-timing toy fit for the teen protagonist.

## Open questions

  Otepää, Viljandi, Pärnu) and its context.
- Date the Town Hall Square dice by stratum (1953 excavation report) to confirm they bracket 1343.

  (chronicles, visitation records, Russow 1578 is too late but may describe older custom).
