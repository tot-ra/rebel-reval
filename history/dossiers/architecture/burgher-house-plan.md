# Burgher house plan (Lower Town, Spring 1343)

## Brief for Map and Art

You are modelling **ordinary Lower Town burgess plots** on 7–11 m strip frontages, not the post-1400 Gothic display facades tourists see today. **In April 1343 timber and stone coexist** on the same plot: street-front houses are **often still wood**, while stone rear houses and cellars are already common on wealthy merchant strips [1][2][12].

**Ship these decisions:**

1. **Massing:** Gable end to street, ridge perpendicular to lane; default **two to three storeys** on merchant front houses, one to two on craft *boda* [1][3][4]. Steep roof pitch (~45° vernacular analogy) [12].
2. **Ground-floor sequence (wealthy merchant / craft front house):** Street → **cellar neck / low terrace** → **diele** (workshop-shop hall) → chimney-kitchen zone → **dornse** (private rear room) → rear yard gate [1][3][5].
3. **Diele:** Largest room; **mantel hearth in a corner** doubles as kitchen and guest reception; may read as **one-and-a-half to two storeys high** in stone houses [3][5]. Street door with external portal; large rectangular ground-floor opening beside it on affluent houses [5].
4. **Dornse:** Ground-floor rear living room; **hypocaust warm-air heat from cellar furnace** on affluent houses — label surviving examples as **plausible composite** for 1343 unless dendro-dated earlier [1][3][6].
5. **Upper floors:** Storage / granary; **loading hatches** on upper storeys with **protruding wooden hoisting beam** above facade openings and interior winch — Hanseatic norm at Reval by mid-century [1][7][8].
6. **Cellar:** Cool warehouse, beer cellar, sometimes inhabited; **cellar necks** at street level before thresholds; on wet plots, raised fill before stone construction [1][2].
7. **Windows (1343-safe):** Ground-floor **pointed-arch stone openings** on stone frontages; **smaller upper openings, often hatches not windows**; avoid late-Gothic four-light crosses and rich blind niches — those are **15th-century onward** on surviving houses [1][9]. Timber houses: smaller glazed or shuttered openings — prevalence **unknown** [12].
8. **Roofs:** Tile on affluent stone/civic masses; **shingle and thatch** on ordinary timber and service buildings; do not tile every roof [12].
9. ***Boda* plot:** Two-room workshop-dwelling, smaller footprint, may be rented from a wealthy burgher; same structural logic, less height and no granary crane [1].
10. **Rear plot:** Servants' buildings, sheds, privy, well, garden; plot walls ~3 m limestone or timber/wattle fences [1][4].

### Cellar, fill, and cellar necks

On unstable wet ground, older wooden buildings were cleared, plots **raised with clay or sand**, then stone construction began [1]. On firmer geology, stone buildings sat on **shallow foundations (~0.5 m)** with limited digging [1]. NUKU-quarter archaeology records a **modest limestone house only at the end of the 14th century**, reinforcing that citywide stone frontage is **not** the 1343 default [12].

- **Cellars** served cool storage and beer cellars; medieval inhabitants sometimes lived in them, but warehouse use dominates in the record [1]. **attested** [1]
- **Cellar necks** (*Kellerhals*) and **low street terraces** before doorways appear in front of Gothic tenement facades [1]. **attested** on surviving houses; universal prevalence in 1343 **plausible composite** [1]
- Town Hall received an **enlarged basement** in the first quarter of the 14th century as part of the diele-dornse establishment — parallel logic for wealthy merchant cellars [10]. **attested** for civic fabric; merchant parallel **plausible composite** [10]

### Diele — front hall / workshop / shop

The **diele** is the street-side ground-floor hall: trade, craft, and guest reception [1][3]. In well-preserved Reval houses it sits **about half a storey higher** than adjoining rooms, which affects threshold steps at the street door [5]. **plausible composite** from surviving layouts; 1343 step heights **unknown** [5]

- **Mantel chimney** in a diele corner served cooking and heating [3][6]. **attested** in surviving interiors [3][6]
- Behind the diele: a **chimney kitchen** and, opposite it, a **stair** rising to a mezzanine/balcony over the dornse [5]. **attested** at Suur-Karja 8 (first written early 15th c.); transfer to 1343 **plausible composite** [5]
- Stone **street portal** and **large rectangular ground-floor window** beside the main door are characteristic of merchant frontages on surviving houses [5][9]. Pointed-arch ground-floor openings are the Gothic stone norm; elaborate carved portals on many tourist houses are **15th c. or later restorations** — do not paste them on every 1343 facade [1][9]

### Dornse — rear living room and heat

The **dornse** is the more private rear ground-floor room, opening toward the yard [1][3]. Affluent houses heat it with a **hypocaust**: a cellar furnace sending warm air through vents to the dornse above [1][3][6]. Three Sisters (Big Sister possibly second half 14th c.) and Pikk 69 retain hypocaust systems in restoration [3][6]. **attested** in surviving fabric; percentage of 1343 houses with hypocaust vs simple hearth **unknown** [3][6]

Upper rear rooms on the first floor served similar representative functions in the Hanseatic scheme [1]. Storage dominated higher floors [1].

### Storey heights and section

Surviving Hanseatic merchant houses present a **narrow, tall street gable** with **two to three occupied storeys** plus steep roof volume [1][3][4]. The diele often reads as a **tall single volume** (double-height effect) opening to two upper storage floors [1][3].

| Level | Typical use | 1343 notes | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cellar | Warehouse, beer, hypocaust furnace | Wet-plot fill may raise floor above natural ground | attested type [1] |
| Ground — diele | Shop, workshop, dining reception | Corner hearth; street-visible activity | attested [1][3] |
| Ground — dornse | Family room, sleeping | Hypocaust on affluent houses | attested / composite [1][3][6] |
| Upper 1–2 | Granary, goods storage | Loading hatches more common than windows | attested [1][7] |
| Attic / roof void | Bulk storage in steep gable | Ventilation hatches | plausible composite [1][7] |

Exact **ceiling heights** for 1343 Reval are **not attested**; use **2.5–3.5 m** clear height per storey as a Lübeck/Riga Hanseatic composite for gameplay scale only [1][11].

### Hoisting beam, winch, and loading hatches

Merchants stored goods on **upper floors and attic**; delivery used **facade loading hatches**, **protruding wooden hoisting beams** above openings, **ropes**, and **interior winches** [1][7][8]. Peppersack (Vana Turg 6; building tradition from **1370s**) shows three storage floors with hatch rhythm [7][8]. Vene 17 (14th-c. merchant fabric) retains hoist-beam evidence [8].

- Practice at Reval: **attested** by surviving houses and Hanseatic function [1][7][8]
- Every front house already equipped in **April 1343**: **plausible composite** — likely on affluent merchant/storage houses, optional on small *boda* [1][7]
- Visible ropes/hooks on tourist houses today: often **later display additions** [7]

### Hearth, chimney, and smoke

- **Diele:** open hearth / mantel chimney for cooking and warmth [3][6]. **attested**
- **Dornse:** hypocaust from cellar; separate chimney mass may rise through diele zone [1][6]. **attested** in stone houses
- **Craft *boda*:** likely single hearth, no hypocaust — **plausible composite** from smaller two-room form [1]
- **Forge and smithy:** hot-work hearth is a separate building type with fire-risk rules — see [`smithy-workshop-layout.md`](smithy-workshop-layout.md) (R-004) [12]

### Windows, facade, and roof treatment

**Stone merchant frontages (1343-safe band):**

- Ground floor: **pointed-arch openings**; wealthy houses may pair **portal + large rectangular window** [1][5]
- Upper floors: **smaller openings**; storage levels often **hatches, not glazed windows** [1]
- **Blind niches, friezes, mouchettes, four-light stone crosses:** late Gothic — **avoid as default in 1343** [1][9]

**Timber frontages:**

- Log, in-fill, or half-timber techniques on tight plots [1]
- Smaller openings; limewash/plaster over timber per project audit [12]
- Do not assign surviving 15th-c. stone Gothic skin to every 1343 timber front [1][12]

**Roofs:**

- **Steep gable** to street; ridge perpendicular to lane [1]
- **Ceramic tile** on affluent stone merchants, civic buildings, and some institutional roofs [1][12]
- **Wood shingle and thatch/reed** on ordinary timber houses and service sheds — ward-level bands in project audit [12]
- By the **15th century** contiguous shared roofing across a plot becomes common; in **1343** treat shared roofs as **possible but not universal** [1]

### Wood vs stone in 1343 — reconcile the sources

Medieval Heritage states that in the **14th century front street houses were mostly wood**, while **square-plan stone houses** often stood behind them on the same plot; stone frontage spreads more widely in the **15th century** [1]. The project audit (H04) agrees: **no defensible citywide 1343 wood-to-stone percentage** — vary by wealth and ward [12].

| Ward bias (April 1343) | Front house | Rear | Roof |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pikk / market merchant | Mixed timber front with stone cellars/rear house common | Stone storehouse, yard sheds | More tile on stone masses [4][12] |
| Viru / craft edge | Timber-dominant | Wattle-fenced yards | More shingle/thatch [4][12] |
| *Boda* renter | Small timber two-room | Minimal | Thatch/shingle [1] |

### Regional power context (who owns the house, not how it is built)

- **German burghers** own most frontage plots and guild-eligible workshops; **Estonians** more often appear as labourers, servants, and *boda* renters without full burgher rights [1][4] — detail in people dossier (R-026).
- **Danish crown** on Toompea; Lower Town houses are burgher property under Lübeck law, not Order barracks [4].
- **1343 siege:** houses are intact inside walls; no licence to show post-war rebuilding fabric [4][16].

## Production hooks

- **Art:** Use the **Interior prop list** and **Reference plates** above before generating facades or room kits. Omit late-Gothic enrichment and modern restaurant fit-out from Peppersack interior refs. Tracked in **A-008** / **A-009**.
- **Map:** Interior footprint on 7–11 m frontage: diele ~55–65% of ground floor depth to street, dornse ~35–45% to yard (plausible composite) [3][11]; plot depth budget from street-plan blocks; rear yard minimum 8–15 m on merchant plots [4]; mark *boda* as smaller single-cell or two-cell module [1]. Tracked in **P0-163** / **P2-067**.
- **Character:** Merchant family lives dornse/rear upper; apprentices/servants may sleep diele loft or rear shed; Estonian labourer likely in rear service building, not guild front room [1][4].
- **Quest / Narrative:** Goods hoisted at curfew risk; hypocaust failure in cold April nights; fire spreading from diele hearth to timber neighbour; council inspection of stone party walls (~3 m plot fences) [1].
- **Dialogue:** *Diele* / *Dornse* as Low German room terms; *Keller* cellar; *Boda* for small rented workshop [1].
- **Dev / systems:** House tier tags: `merchant_stone`, `merchant_timber`, `craft_boda`; storage capacity scales with upper floors; hoist action requires winch + hatch on merchant tier only; hypocaust gives warmth bonus to dornse in cold weather [1][7].
